The dark web is the part of the internet that search engines do not index. Much of it hosts marketplaces and forums where data stolen in breaches is sold or shared: email addresses, passwords, phone numbers, home addresses, and financial details. A dark web scan checks those sources for data tied to a client's domains and people.

## What Telivy finds

Every Telivy assessment includes a dark web scan of the client's domains. Results appear under **Dark Web** on the assessment's **Security** tab, and the **Dark Web Presence** card on the **Overview** summarizes them. A leaked record can include email addresses, passwords, usernames, names, phone numbers, physical addresses, IP addresses, and more sensitive data such as dates of birth or government IDs.

### Summary and What we checked

At the top of the **Dark Web** view, a summary shows how many breaches were found, how many of the checked email addresses were exposed, and how many of those had a leaked password. It also shows when the search last ran. Bars below the summary count breaches by year, and clicking a year filters the table to that year.

**What we checked** opens a list of every email address Telivy searched for, grouped by domain, with the number of breach sources searched and how many breaches each address appeared in. If some searches did not finish, the summary is marked **Partly complete** and the dialog says that a few results may be missing until the next scan.

### Breaches table

The **Breaches** table has one row per breach, with the people affected and a **Risk** rating. You can search it by breach name or email address. Passwords are partly hidden unless you turn on **Show full passwords**, which applies to that client only. The same setting appears as **Mask Dark Web Passwords** in the assessment's PII settings. See [Data Security](/products/risk-assessments/data-security#mask-pii-data).

Select a breach to open its details:

| Section | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Header | The breach name, its date, how many accounts it affected, and its **Risk** rating. Labels such as **Not confirmed**, **Spam list**, **Built from older breaches**, or **Stolen by malware** describe the source when they apply. |
| What happened | A short description of the breach. |
| What leaked | The types of data exposed. Passwords, Social Security numbers, dates of birth, and government IDs are called out with what an attacker can do with them. |
| Who's affected | The client's email addresses that appear in the breach. |
| What to do | Numbered steps to remediate. |

### Adding email addresses with the Dark Web Email List

Telivy finds most of the email addresses it checks on its own, from public sources for the client's domain and, on a Risk Assessment with Microsoft 365 connected, from the tenant's users. Addresses it cannot discover, such as contractors, shared mailboxes, or employees' personal addresses, can be added with the **Dark Web Email List** on a Risk Assessment.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open the step">
    On the Risk Assessment's **Setup** tab, go to **5. Optional Steps > Dark Web Email List**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Prepare the CSV">
    Select **Download CSV Template**. The file has three columns: `first_name`, `last_name`, and `email`. Fill in one person per row. The upload is rejected if any of the three column headers is missing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Upload it">
    Drop the file on **Upload CSV file here**, or select it to browse. When the upload finishes, the file name appears with a check mark.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The upload adds addresses to the assessment. Addresses already on it are skipped, and uploading another file does not remove earlier ones. Telivy checks each new address against breach data straight away and starts a new external scan, so the uploaded addresses appear in **What we checked** and in the **Breaches** table along with the ones Telivy found itself.

On a Risk Assessment, Telivy also checks the passwords saved in employees' browsers against breach data and lists matches under **Browser Credentials on Dark Web** on the **Identity and Access Management** view. See [Identity and Access Management](/products/risk-assessments/identity-and-access-management).

## Why it matters

Stolen credentials are the cheapest way into a company. Attackers try leaked email and password pairs against Microsoft 365, VPNs, and other logins, and they work whenever someone reused a password or never changed it. A leaked email address with a name and phone number is also enough to build a convincing phishing message.

A dark web scan tells you which accounts are already exposed, so you can reset them before someone uses them. For a prospect, it turns a general warning into a list of their own exposed accounts.

## How severity is judged

Telivy gives each breach a **Risk** rating of High, Medium, or Low from what leaked and when the breach happened:

| Rating | When it applies |
| --- | --- |
| High | A cleartext password leaked, or the breach happened in the last 24 months. |
| Medium | A hashed password leaked, or the breach happened between 24 and 48 months ago. |
| Low | None of the above applies, for example a breach older than 48 months, or one with no known date, that exposed no password. |

A breach that Telivy's sources mark as a spam list or as fabricated is rated Low unless a cleartext password leaked, whatever its date.

A breach row takes the highest rating among the records in it.

The breach dates also affect the client's grade. Telivy grades a client lower when leaks are recent. Findings from the last two years count most, and leaks older than five years count least. See [Telivy Grading Criteria](/scoring/grading-criteria).

When you decide which accounts to fix first, also look at whose account it is and whether the password is still in use. An administrator or finance account matters more than a former intern's, and a leaked password that matches one still saved in a browser is an active risk.

## How leaked data gets used

Leaked credentials lead to account takeover through credential stuffing, where attackers try the same password on many services. Leaked personal details feed targeted phishing and business email compromise, and both can end in fraud, ransomware, or more data theft. A company that appears in one breach is also more likely to be targeted again.

## Remediation

1. Reset the password for every account with a leaked password, and anywhere else the same password was used.
2. Turn on MFA for those accounts, starting with administrators and anyone who handles money.
3. Check sign-in logs for the affected accounts for unusual locations or failed attempts. For Microsoft 365 clients, the [Microsoft 365 Security](/products/risk-assessments/microsoft-365-security) view shows these.
4. Tell the affected users, and use the finding in security awareness training so people recognize the phishing that usually follows a leak.
5. Keep scanning. New breaches surface all the time, and [Risk Monitoring](/products/risk-monitoring/overview) alerts you when new leaks appear.

<Note>
  Do not try to access dark web marketplaces yourself to check a client's data. Much of what happens there is illegal, and visiting carries real legal and security risk. Telivy collects the data for you.
</Note>
