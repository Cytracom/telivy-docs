Every open port on an internet-facing system is a way in. Some have to be open, like HTTPS
on a web server. Many are left open by accident, and those are what attackers scan for.

## What ports are

A port is a numbered endpoint on a device, and each one is tied to a service. Port 443
carries HTTPS web traffic, port 22 carries SSH, and so on. A **Telivy External Assessment**
or a **Risk Assessment** lists the ports on a company's systems that can be reached from
the internet.

## Why open ports are a risk

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="A bigger attack surface">
    Each open port is one more service an attacker can probe. The more ports are open, the
    more chances there are that one of them runs something vulnerable or misconfigured.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Known vulnerabilities in the service behind the port">
    Attackers scan the internet for specific open ports and then try known exploits against
    the service running there. Unpatched software or an outdated configuration makes that
    much easier.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="A route for malware">
    A vulnerable service on an open port can be used to install malware, which can lead to
    data theft or an attacker taking control of the device.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## High-risk ports: close unless absolutely necessary

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="RPC Endpoint Mapper / NetBIOS (135)">
    Windows uses it to locate RPC services and for legacy NetBIOS name resolution. It is a
    common target for remote exploits and worms. Restrict it to trusted networks, or turn it
    off if nothing needs it.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Remote Desktop (3389)">
    Gives remote access to a Windows desktop. If it has to be reachable, it needs strong
    passwords and careful configuration. A VPN or a dedicated remote access tool in front of
    it is safer.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Database ports (MySQL 3306, PostgreSQL 5432)">
    Used to connect to a database. Avoid exposing them to the internet. If you must, enforce
    access controls, strong passwords, and MFA.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Medium-risk ports: use with caution

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="NetBIOS / SMB (137, 139)">
    Used for file and printer sharing on older Windows systems, and often abused by malware
    to move between machines. Turn them off if they aren't needed, or limit them to trusted
    internal networks.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Low-risk ports: lower priority, still worth reviewing

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="FTP (21)">
    Transfers files without encryption. Telivy rates an open FTP port as low severity, but
    it's still worth replacing with SFTP (22) or FTPS (990).
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Telnet (23)">
    Remote access without encryption. Telivy rates it low severity, but SSH (22) is the
    safer choice.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## How Telivy scores open ports

In an External Assessment, each open port Telivy finds is matched against the lists above
and gets that severity (high, medium, or low), based on the service most commonly
associated with the port.

A Risk Assessment, which scans devices through the agent, works differently. Open ports
found on a device are reported together as a single **Device Open Ports Detected** finding
with one severity, instead of being scored port by port.

Some common ports aren't in the severity lists at all. SSH (22), DNS (53), HTTP (80), and
HTTPS (443) are expected to be open on most servers and aren't flagged as open-port findings
on their own. SMTP (25) is checked separately: Telivy looks at whether the mail service uses
SSL/TLS encryption and reports missing encryption as its own finding.

## How to close unused ports

How you close a port depends on the device or firewall. Start with the list of open ports
from the Telivy scan. For each one, work out which service is listening and whether anyone
still needs it. If not, stop the service or block the port at the firewall. If it is
needed, restrict who can reach it, for example to a VPN or a list of trusted IP addresses.
Ports opened for things like games or old VPN setups are worth checking too, since they are
often forgotten once the reason for them goes away.
